3. Remaining major problems
Issue 1 — The abstract and formal method still overclaim metric blindness
High priority
This is the biggest conceptual problem still present.
In Section 4.1, the paper states:
Near-0.5 AUROC means P carries no information about F.

It then concludes that the evaluation metric cannot reward or penalize that mechanism.
This statement is still too strong. Near-chance AUROC shows weak marginal rank discrimination, not complete statistical independence or zero influence on a downstream ranking metric.
The new Section 5.8 intervention experiment substantially helps. But a flat top-50 metric across five tested penalty strengths only establishes local insensitivity over those strengths, dataset splits, and metrics.
It does not prove that the metric cannot detect the mechanism at all.
Recommended correction: Reframe the alignment check as a screening diagnostic and the intervention experiment as its operational validation. Make it explicit that AUROC is neither a necessary nor sufficient condition for ranking-metric insensitivity.
This is especially important because Section 5.7 already makes a more cautious statement, while the abstract, introduction, and Section 4.1 retain categorical language.
Issue 2 — The synthetic experiment's most important conclusion needs another test
High priority
Section 5.8 reports a particularly interesting result.
Synthetic ground-truth experiment
Measure	Naive	Proposed
Confound discrimination (AUROC)	0.68	0.83
AUPRC	0.302	0.482
Top-50 high-confound rate	0.480	0.676
Source: revised manuscript, Section 5.8. Plain-model top-50 rate is 0.656.
The paper interprets these results as evidence that the proposed method identifies the confound better but fails to remove it from the ranking.
That interpretation is plausible, but reviewers may challenge the following distinction:
- The estimated factor appears to predict the simulated confound effectively.
- The actual correction term, derived through model coefficients and factor projection, may not preserve that predictive relationship.
- The final ranking may also retain confounding through the chemistry representation, even after the explicit factor contribution is removed.
To isolate these explanations, I would test three alternatives using the same synthetic data:
1. Subtract a calibrated version of the estimated factor directly from the baseline score.
2. Fit the model after removing the estimated confound contribution from its representation, rather than dropping coefficients only during scoring.
3. Compare both approaches to an oracle correction using the known simulated confound.
This could turn the paper's most intriguing empirical observation into a much stronger methodological result.
Issue 3 — The out-of-sample factor implementation remains conceptually important
Major
Section 5.7 now explains that the factor is estimated for test compounds through a linear projection from the base representation, achieving cross-validated \(R^2\) of 0.44–0.56.
This fixes a serious implementation problem in the previous version.
However, it introduces another question: how much of the estimated factor is simply reconstructed from the original features?
If the test factor is predicted as
\[
\widehat A_{\mathrm{test}}=X_{\mathrm{test}}B,
\]

then its values are necessarily functions of the base representation.
This makes the exact training and scoring equations crucial. The manuscript should specify whether the held-out scores come from a jointly fitted model and how the estimated factor changes the final coefficient contributions.
I would also report the alignment results using several valid out-of-sample factor estimators to determine whether the findings depend heavily on the selected linear projection.

Major
The new intervention experiment is valuable, but its presentation is too compressed for the importance of its conclusions.

It also describes \(\alpha=1\) as the normal naive correction and \(\alpha=0\) as the normal proposed correction. This is not intuitive without writing the two scoring functions explicitly.

Issue 5 — A potential cold-start leakage problem still needs clarification
High priority
Section 4 now says that matrix-factorization gradients are masked to training cells, preventing held-out labels from entering the shared embedding.
That is an improvement, but masking labels does not explain how a free matrix-factorization embedding is obtained for completely unseen drugs under CVS2.
If a held-out drug has no training interactions, its free embedding cannot be reliably learned from the interaction matrix alone without some additional feature-to-embedding mapping or cold-start procedure.
The methods must explain precisely how this is handled.
Otherwise, a reviewer may question whether the cold-start evaluation is valid, even if label leakage has been prevented.
Issue 6 — The statistical evidence is still insufficient
Major
The new synthetic experiment uses five seeds, but Section 5.8 largely reports means without uncertainty intervals.
Likewise, Table 3 no longer reports the standard deviations that were present in the earlier manuscript.
Five seeds are useful for an initial experiment, but I would recommend 20–30 synthetic replicates, paired differences, and confidence intervals for the most important claims.
One smaller methodological concern: Table 3 uses max(AUROC, 1 − AUROC) to make the results sign-free. This can create an upward bias even for uninformative scores. If direction is genuinely arbitrary, establish it using training data rather than selecting the better orientation on the evaluation set.

5. How I would position this paper for RECOMB
Right now, the manuscript offers two related findings:
- Evaluation alignment: Bias diagnostics can respond differently to corrections because their measured quantities differ.
- Correction-application gap: A method can estimate a confounding factor relatively well without successfully removing its influence from the final prediction.
I think the second finding could become the stronger central contribution, especially if you demonstrate why the fit-then-project-out strategy fails under identifiable conditions.
The first can then serve as a diagnostic framework for discovering and characterizing that failure.
This positioning would fit RECOMB's emphasis on substantial computational or methodological advances rather than simply applying existing methods to new datasets.
