## 2. Dataset standardness: ChEMBL, SIDER, ATC

This was flagged as a hard requirement, so each dataset is confirmed separately with citing papers actually found during this review (not assumed from memory).

### ChEMBL (indications + max clinical trial phase)
Confirmed as a standard, public, FAIR, EBI-maintained resource, independent of this project.
- Gaulton et al., "The ChEMBL database in 2017," *Nucleic Acids Research* (PMC5210557).
- Zdrazil et al., "The ChEMBL Database in 2023: a drug discovery platform spanning multiple bioactivity data types and time periods," *Nucleic Acids Research* (PMC10767899).
- "Drug and Clinical Candidate Drug Data in ChEMBL," *J. Med. Chem.* 2025 (PMC12516679) — explicitly documents ChEMBL's curation of drugs and clinical-candidate progression (i.e., max phase), which is the exact field the project plans to use.
- "Data-driven strategies for drug repurposing: insights, recommendations, and case studies," *Brief. Bioinform.* 2025 (PMC12641610) — a repurposing-methods review that treats ChEMBL as one of the standard integrated data sources.

### SIDER (side effects, and indications)
Confirmed public and standard; actively used both as a predictive feature source and as ground truth/negative-control material in repurposing papers.
- Kuhn, Letunic, Jensen, Bork, "The SIDER database of drugs and side effects," *Nucleic Acids Research* 2016 (PMC4702794) — current reference version.
- Kuhn et al., "A side effect resource to capture phenotypic effects of drugs," *Mol. Syst. Biol.* 2010 (PMC2824526) — original SIDER release.
- Campillos et al., "Drug target identification using side-effect similarity," *Science* 2008 — the landmark repurposing use of SIDER-type phenotypic data.
- Azuma, Mizuno, Kusuhara, NRBdMF (arXiv:2208.04312, 2022/2023) — uses SIDER indications and side effects directly as signed (+1/−1) training labels, which is methodologically the nearest precedent to the hard-negative design proposed here.

### ATC (Anatomical Therapeutic Chemical classification)
Confirmed standard and widely used as both a label to predict and a covariate/grouping variable in repurposing papers; at least seven independent groups were found using ATC Level 1–5 for repurposing-adjacent tasks, for example:
- "Deep fusion learning facilitates anatomical therapeutic chemical recognition in drug repurposing and discovery," *Brief. Bioinform.* 2021.
- "Drug repositioning by prediction of drug's anatomical therapeutic chemical code via network-based inference approaches," *Brief. Bioinform.* 2020.
- "iATC-NRAKEL: an efficient multi-label classifier for recognizing anatomical therapeutic chemical classes of drugs," *Bioinformatics* 2020.
- "FACT: Feature Aggregation and Convolution with Transformers for predicting drug classification code," *Bioinformatics* 2025.

**Bottom line:** all three datasets are standard, public, and already load-bearing in the peer-reviewed drug-repurposing literature. A reviewer cannot credibly reject this trio as "invented." The weaker link, discussed next, is not ChEMBL/SIDER/ATC but the 43-proconvulsant list.

Two additional benchmark resources worth citing for evaluation credibility (see §4): repoDB (Brown & Patel, *Scientific Data* 2017) and the Broad Institute Drug Repurposing Hub (Corsello et al., *Nature Medicine* 2017) — the latter is also the current source of the name-matched "therapeutic area" label the project plans to replace with ATC.

## 3. PACT and the "43 proconvulsants": findings and recommendation

**What PACT is.** I located PACT directly in the project's own files (`/mnt/c/Users/rishik/epilepsy_repurpose/PACT/`). It is **not** a published or publicly known pharmacovigilance resource, and it is not a recognized acronym in epilepsy/pharmacology — general web searches for "PACT" + proconvulsant/pharmacovigilance returned nothing matching. PACT stands for **"Proconvulsant-Anchored Comparative Testbed"**, an internal project by the same author (R. Kondadadi), documented as a short IEEE-formatted conference abstract: *"Antiseizure Medications Are Transcriptomically Invisible: A Detectability Limit for Drug Repurposing,"* submitted to what the file names as "Great Plains 2026" (a regional conference; I could not independently verify its indexing status, selectivity, or whether it is archival). This is self-citation territory, not an external public dataset.

**Where the 43 compounds actually come from.** The PACT README states explicitly: *"Proconvulsant labels are literature-curated (Chen et al. 2016), not FAERS disproportionality-derived."* Chen, Albertson & Olson, "Treatment of drug-induced seizures," *Br. J. Clin. Pharmacol.* 2016 (81(3):412–419), is a real, peer-reviewed clinical review of drugs that cause seizures — I confirmed its existence and content independently via web search. So the provenance chain is: **PACT (unpublished internal abstract) → curated against Chen et al. 2016 (published, public) → 43 LINCS-profiled compounds**. The list is not fabricated from nothing, but it is also not itself a public, independently citable dataset; it is this team's own unpublished curation step layered on top of a public source.

**Risk for RECOMB reviewers.** Citing "PACT's curated list of 43 proconvulsants" as if it were an established external resource would be inaccurate and could read as inventing a dataset, exactly the failure mode the task flagged. A reviewer who checks will find a self-authored, non-archival conference abstract, not a recognized pharmacovigilance product (unlike, say, FAERS, SIDER, or OffSIDES).

**Public alternatives/supplements found:**
- Ruffmann, Bogliun & Beghi, "Epileptogenic drugs: a systematic review," *Expert Rev. Neurother.* 2006 — a peer-reviewed, risk-tiered list of epileptogenic drugs (high: meperidine, sevoflurane, clozapine, phenothiazines, cyclosporine; intermediate: propofol, maprotiline, tricyclic antidepressants, chlorambucil; low: fluoroquinolones, carbapenems, bupropion, iodinated contrast).
- Shorvon, Tallis & Wallace, *J. Neurol. Neurosurg. Psychiatry* 2002 — epidemiological study of proconvulsant drug co-prescription in a real epilepsy population (General Practice Research Database), useful as external validation that the named drug classes are clinically recognized as proconvulsant, not just pharmacologically asserted.
- SIDER's own "convulsion" / "seizure" MedDRA preferred-term entries — a fully public, already-in-hand substitute or cross-check: any drug with SIDER-coded convulsion/seizure as an adverse event is an independently sourced, machine-readable proconvulsant flag, with no dependence on an unpublished internal list.
- FDA Adverse Event Reporting System (FAERS) seizure/convulsion signals were explicitly *not* used by PACT (per its own README) and were not independently re-investigated in this review, but remain a plausible public alternative if the team wants a second, non-SIDER-sourced check.

**Recommendation: supplement, don't just keep.** Do not present the 43-compound list as drawn from an established external resource ("PACT") without disclosure. Three options, in order of rigor:
1. **Minimum fix:** cite the list honestly as the authors' own prior curation (self-citation to the PACT abstract, if it is submitted/accepted by paper time), explicitly grounded in Chen et al. 2016, and state the curation criteria (which of Chen et al.'s reported drugs were LINCS-profiled).
2. **Stronger fix (recommended):** cross-validate or expand the 43-compound list against SIDER's convulsion/seizure terms and the Ruffmann et al. 2006 risk tiers, and report the overlap. This converts an internal list into one with two independent, fully public corroborating sources, which should satisfy a RECOMB reviewer's "is this dataset real" check.
3. **Belt-and-suspenders:** also check FAERS for seizure/convulsion disproportionality on the same 43 drugs as a third, independent public signal, even though PACT deliberately avoided this for its own reasons (likely to avoid reporting-bias confounds); for a *validation* cross-check rather than the primary label source, that concern is less severe.

## 4. Novelty gap: "no repurposing method uses negative-control indications"

**Assessment: plausible but narrower than the paper states, and needs careful scoping.** Based on the searches conducted here:

- No paper was found that uses the specific construct **"negative-control indications"** (diseases in the same organ system, treated by a different mechanism, used explicitly to estimate and remove a therapeutic-area latent factor) inside a drug-repurposing or DTI model. This part of the claim held up across targeted searches combining "negative control(s)," "drug repositioning/repurposing," "matrix factorization," and "debiasing."
- The closest prior work clusters into three distinct lineages, none of which does exactly this:
  1. **NRBdMF** (Azuma et al., arXiv:2208.04312) uses signed labels from SIDER (indication = −1, side effect = +1) within a single drug's own profile to reduce false positives. This is a *within-drug* bidirectional signal, not a *cross-indication* negative-control-disease signal used to estimate a shared confound. It is the single closest method and must be discussed carefully; the paper should state precisely how its mechanism differs (NRBdMF never estimates or removes a latent therapeutic-area factor; it never uses *other diseases* as controls).
  2. **DTI/repurposing debiasing papers** (TAPB, *Nat. Commun.* 2025; DebiasedDTA, *CMB* 2023; DRDM, *J. Biomed. Inform.* 2025; "Escaping the Drug-Bias Trap," *TCBBIO* 2025) address popularity bias, target-prior bias, or dataset-structure bias generically, via reweighting, interventions, or ensemble balancing — not via negative-control diseases, and generally not framed around therapeutic-area/organ-system confounding specifically.
  3. **Recommender-systems causal debiasing** (inverse-propensity scoring, counterfactual learning, confounder balancing: e.g., "Debiased Recommendation with User Feature Balancing," arXiv:2201.06056) is the general machine-learning analogue of "there's a latent factor correlated with both exposure and outcome that needs removing," but again without a negative-control-based identification strategy and outside the drug-repurposing domain.
- **A genuine prior-art risk the paper should pre-empt:** the general statistical idea of "fit shared latent factors across related tasks, then use known-null tasks to identify and remove them" is *not new in statistics* — it is exactly RUV/SVA (genomics) and negative-control-outcome calibration (pharmacoepidemiology, including the large-scale empirical calibration work in observational-database epidemiology that fits many simultaneous negative-control estimates to calibrate a target estimate). The novelty claim should therefore be scoped precisely to: *"no prior drug-repurposing/DTI method has used negative-control indications, specifically, to identify and remove an organ-system/therapeutic-area latent confound in a cross-indication model,"* not to negative controls or RUV-style decomposition in general, since both exist abundantly outside the repurposing literature and the paper is explicitly borrowing from them. Overclaiming novelty relative to the statistics literature (rather than just the repurposing literature) is the likeliest reviewer objection.
- I could not fully verify "DRDM" (*J. Biomed. Inform.* 2025) and "Escaping the Drug-Bias Trap" beyond their abstracts in this review; before submission, the team should read both full texts to make certain neither already does something closer to the proposed negative-control-indication mechanism than the abstracts suggest.

## 5. Recommended evaluation protocol

Drawing on what is standard across the matrix-factorization repurposing literature (NRLMF/NRBdMF-style CV schemes) and the repoDB/Repurposing-Hub benchmarking tradition:

1. **Benchmark backbone.** Evaluate on the full ChEMBL indication matrix (primary) and report a secondary comparison against repoDB's curated true-positive/true-negative drug-disease pairs, since repoDB is the closest thing the field has to an accepted gold standard and reviewers will expect it or an explanation for its absence.
2. **Cross-validation scheme, matched to the literature.** Use the CVS1/CVS2/CVS3 convention common in DTI/repurposing matrix factorization (NRLMF, NRBdMF, and the matrix-factorization survey literature all report these): CVS1 = random pair holdout, CVS2 = entire-drug holdout (cold-start drug), CVS3 = entire-disease holdout (cold-start disease). The area-debiasing claim should be checked separately under all three, since a shared therapeutic-area factor could leak information across folds especially in CVS1.
3. **Scaffold/structure-aware splits for chemistry-based baselines.** Since the paper is isolating a mechanism-vs-structure confound, any ECFP4-based or molecular baseline needs a Tanimoto-similarity-based (scaffold-disjoint) split, not a random split, exactly as the PACT abstract itself already does — this is good practice to carry forward.
4. **Primary bias-diagnostic metrics.** Report, per indication (with depression/antipsychotics as primary, epilepsy secondary, matching the stated confound strength ordering): (a) AUPRC on true indications, (b) the top-k false-positive therapeutic-area enrichment statistic already used in the motivating analysis (the 38%/14% and 32%/base-rate comparisons), before and after debiasing, (c) ranking of known hard negatives (SIDER convulsion/hypotension-flagged drugs, and the proconvulsant panel) — these should fall, not rise, post-debiasing.
5. **Negative-control validity checks, imported directly from the pharmacoepidemiology literature.** Before trusting the area-factor estimate, run the standard negative-control diagnostic: the fitted model should show *no* residual association between the negative-control indications themselves (same organ system, different mechanism) after the correction, analogous to a calibrated null distribution in Lipsitch/Tchetgen Tchetgen-style negative-control analysis. Report this as a calibration plot, not just an aggregate score.
6. **Ablations against the two existing identification strategies.** (a) the naive "subtract generic area-ness score" baseline already reported (AUPRC 0.45 → 0.17) as the lower-bound failure mode, and (b) an NRBdMF-style within-drug signed-label baseline, to make the novelty-gap argument empirically rather than just rhetorically.
7. **External/temporal validation.** If feasible, hold out indications or ChEMBL max-phase updates by approval/trial date (train on pre-cutoff indications, test on later ones) to show the method is not just fitting historical confounds; this is increasingly expected in repurposing papers given known data-leakage criticisms of static split evaluation (cf. "Towards a more inductive world for drug repurposing approaches," arXiv:2311.12670, which explicitly critiques transductive-only evaluation in this space).

## 6. Caveats on this review

- All numeric claims about the proposed method (AUPRC values, 38%/14% and 32% enrichment figures, the epilepsy 18%/15% figures) are taken as given from the task description and were not independently re-derived or verified here; this review covers the *literature*, not the project's own internal results.
- "DRDM" (J. Biomed. Inform. 2025) and "Escaping the Drug-Bias Trap" (TCBBIO 2025) were assessed from abstracts only; full-text review is recommended before the novelty claim is finalized.
- NRBdMF's peer-reviewed publication venue (beyond the arXiv preprint, arXiv:2208.04312) could not be confirmed in this search; cite it as an arXiv preprint unless the team can locate a journal DOI.
- The Great Plains 2026 abstract's venue status (archival vs. non-archival, peer-review depth) was not independently verifiable from public sources; treat any self-citation to it accordingly.
